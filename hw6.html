HW 06 · Statistical independence

Alessandro Bertoldi · Statistics in Cybersecurity. From a bivariate dataset (X, F) to the contingency table, marginal and conditional frequencies, expected counts under independence, the χ² statistic and its p-value.

01Independence: theory in brief

  • Events. A and B are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A) when P(B) > 0: knowing B does not change the probability of A.
  • Discrete variables. X and F are independent if and only if the joint pmf factorizes into the marginals, pij = pi··p·j for every pair (i, j). Equivalently every conditional distribution P(F | X = xᵢ) equals the marginal P(F): the rows of the contingency table are proportional.
  • Expected counts under independence. Eij = n·pi··p·j = Ri·Cj/n, where Ri and Cj are the row and column totals and n the sample size.
χ² = Σᵢ Σⱼ (Oij − Eij)² / Eij df = (r − 1)(c − 1) p-value = P(χ²df ≥ observed | H₀) Cramér's V = √( χ² / ( n · min(r − 1, c − 1) ) ) adjusted residual = (O − E) / √( E (1 − R/n)(1 − C/n) )

H₀: X and F are independent; H₁: they are associated (in any way). Under H₀ the statistic is approximately χ² distributed with df degrees of freedom (the marginals are estimated from the data, so df = rc − 1 − (r − 1) − (c − 1)). Conditions: independent observations, and expected counts that are not too small (rule of thumb: all E ≥ 1 and at least 80% of them ≥ 5; otherwise use exact or Monte Carlo tests). The p-value is computed from the regularized incomplete gamma function, Q(df/2, χ²/2), implemented from scratch. As an optional extra, a permutation test shuffles the F labels and gives a p-value without the asymptotic approximation.

04Interpretation: association is not causation

  • What the test says. A small p-value (below α) is evidence against independence: the observed table would be unlikely if X and F were independent. A large p-value only says that the data are compatible with independence; it does not prove it, because the sample may be too small to detect a real association (low power).
  • Significance is not importance. With large n a tiny deviation becomes significant. Cramér's V measures the strength: about 0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large (for 2×2 tables). The third dataset is significant yet nearly negligible; the first one is both significant and moderately strong.
  • Where the dependence comes from. The adjusted residuals show which cells have more (positive) or fewer (negative) observations than independence predicts; values beyond about ±2 are notable.
  • Association vs causality. Statistical dependence says only that the variables move together. It does not say that X causes F. A confounder can drive both (employees who got training may also work in teams with better security practices), the direction can be reversed (organisations that suffered incidents may then invest in training), and Simpson's paradox can make an association appear, vanish or flip when a third variable is taken into account. Causal claims require a randomized experiment or an explicit causal model; an observational χ² test cannot provide them. Conversely, independence in a table is not proof of no causal effect either (effects can cancel across subgroups).
  • Other cautions. The test assumes independent records and an adequate sample; continuous variables must be binned (which loses information); testing many pairs of variables inflates false positives.
