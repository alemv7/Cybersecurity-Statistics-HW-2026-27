HW 04 · Welford and sampling statistics

Alessandro Bertoldi · Statistics in Cybersecurity. Exercise 1: online recurrences for mean, sum of squares and variance. Exercise 2: m hackers, n systems, scores in {0,…,10}.

01Exercise 1 · Derivation of Welford's recurrences

Notation: after k observations, Mk = (1/k)·Σi≤k xᵢ is the running mean, SSk = Σi≤k (xᵢ − Mk)² the sum of squares about the mean, M₀ = SS₀ = 0.

Knuth's recurrence for the mean

M_k = ((k−1)·M_{k−1} + x_k) / k = M_{k−1} + (x_k − M_{k−1}) / k

Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares

Let d = x_k − M_{k−1}. From Knuth, M_k = M_{k−1} + d/k. Split SSk into the first k−1 terms and the last one:

SS_k = Σ_{i<k} (x_i − M_{k−1} − d/k)² + (x_k − M_k)² First sum: Σ_{i<k}(x_i − M_{k−1})² − (2d/k)·Σ_{i<k}(x_i − M_{k−1}) + (k−1)·d²/k² = SS_{k−1} − 0 + (k−1)·d²/k² (the deviations from the mean sum to 0) Last term: x_k − M_k = d − d/k = (k−1)·d/k, so (x_k − M_k)² = (k−1)²·d²/k² Adding: SS_k = SS_{k−1} + (k−1)·d²/k² + (k−1)²·d²/k² = SS_{k−1} + (k−1)·d²·(1 + k − 1)/k² = SS_{k−1} + (k−1)·d²/k

Since x_k − M_k = (k−1)·d/k, we have (k−1)·d²/k = d·(x_k − M_k), so no factor k is needed in the code:

SS_k = SS_{k−1} + (x_k − M_{k−1})·(x_k − M_k)

Recurrence for the variance

sample variance s²_k = SS_k / (k−1) (k ≥ 2) (k−1)·s²_k = (k−2)·s²_{k−1} + (k−1)·d²/k s²_k = ((k−2)/(k−1))·s²_{k−1} + (x_k − M_{k−1})² / k population-style variance SS_k / k (biased: E = ((k−1)/k)·σ²)

The code: core logic in 3 lines

delta = x - mean          # x_k − M_{k−1}
mean += delta / k         # Knuth:  M_k
ss   += delta * (x - mean)  # Welford: SS_k = SS_{k−1} + (x_k − M_{k−1})(x_k − M_k)

Full loop in Python: for k, x in enumerate(data, 1): delta = x - mean; mean += delta / k; ss += delta * (x - mean), then var = ss / (k - 1). It uses O(1) memory, one pass, and avoids the cancellation of the textbook formula SS = Σx² − (Σx)²/n.

02Exercise 2 · m hackers, n systems

Each attempt receives an integer score in {0,…,10} drawn i.i.d. from a fixed distribution, with theoretical mean μ and variance σ² computed from its pmf. Each of the m hackers produces n scores; their sampling mean and sampling variance (divisor n−1) are computed with Welford's recurrences, then averaged over the hackers. For each n the experiment is repeated with fresh scores.

03Conclusions

  • Averages versus theory. The average of the sampling means matches μ and the average of the sampling variances (divisor n−1) matches σ² for every n, within the Monte Carlo error: the z-scores in the tables are mostly inside ±2 or ±3. So the sample mean and the sample variance are unbiased estimators: E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ². The version with divisor n is biased: its average is ((n−1)/n)·σ², visibly too low for small n, and the table confirms it.
  • Why it matters in inferential statistics. The parameters μ and σ² of a population (here the attacker's skill and consistency) are unknown and one only sees a sample. Unbiasedness means no systematic error; the standard error σ/√n of the mean gives confidence intervals and tests; consistency means more data brings the estimate to the truth. Welford's formulas give these estimates online, in one pass and with numerical stability, which suits streaming security logs.
  • Effect of n. Unbiasedness does not depend on n, but variability does. The standard deviation of the sampling means follows σ/√n (it halves when n is multiplied by 4), and the distribution of the sampling means becomes normal as in the CLT. The sampling variance also concentrates: Var(s²) = (μ₄ − σ⁴(n−3)/(n−1))/n ∝ 1/n, so its standard deviation also shrinks like 1/√n, though from a skewed shape for small n. Larger n therefore makes each hacker's estimates more reliable, while m only improves how precisely we check the averages (error ∝ 1/√m).
