Part A · Modes of convergence

01The four modes

Let X₁, X₂, … and X be random variables on the same probability space (for convergence in distribution only the laws matter).

Counterexamples (section 04): the typewriter sequence converges in probability and in L² but not a.s.; Xₙ = n·1{U < 1/n} converges a.s. and in probability but not in L²; Xₙ = −X with X symmetric converges in distribution to X but not in probability.

02Their roles in the LLN and the CLT

  • Weak LLN (convergence in probability): for i.i.d. variables with mean μ, X̄ₙ →ᴾ μ. With finite variance it follows from Chebyshev: P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²). This is consistency of the sample mean as an estimator.
  • Mean-square version: E(X̄ₙ − μ)² = σ²/n → 0, so X̄ₙ →ᴸ² μ, which implies the weak LLN and quantifies the rate (mean squared error).
  • Strong LLN (almost sure; Kolmogorov, needs only E|X| < ∞): with probability 1 the running average of one infinite sequence of observations converges to μ. This is the justification for estimating a long-run frequency or mean from a single long record, and for Monte Carlo.
  • CLT (convergence in distribution): √n (X̄ₙ − μ)/σ →ᵈ N(0, 1) when σ² < ∞. It describes the shape and scale of the error, not that the error vanishes: it is the basis of confidence intervals and tests. Because the limit of X̄ₙ is a constant, X̄ₙ →ᵈ μ also gives X̄ₙ →ᴾ μ; the CLT is a finer statement than the LLN about the same average.
  • Other results. Slutsky's theorem and the continuous-mapping theorem (combining →ᴾ and →ᵈ, e.g. replacing σ by s in the t-statistic); the delta method; Glivenko–Cantelli (empirical CDF converges a.s. uniformly); asymptotic normality of the MLE, √n(θ̂ − θ) →ᵈ N(0, 1/I(θ)); the law of the iterated logarithm (a.s. statement about fluctuations of size √(2n log log n)).

03Simulation: the LLN in probability, a.s. and L²

Each of m trajectories of i.i.d. draws gives running means X̄ₖ. We estimate three quantities at several n: P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) (in probability); P(supn≤k≤N |X̄ₖ − μ| > ε) (the tail event of a.s. convergence, truncated at the horizon N); and E(X̄ₙ − μ)² (L²). The Cauchy law has no mean: the LLN fails and none of the three decreases.

04Counterexamples: why the modes differ

(i) Typewriter sequence on Ω = [0,1) with uniform P: in block k, the indicators of the k intervals [(j−1)/k, j/k) are listed one after the other. Then P(Xₙ = 1) = 1/k → 0 and E Xₙ² = 1/k → 0 (convergence to 0 in probability and in L²), but every ω lands in exactly one interval of every block, so Xₙ(ω) = 1 infinitely often: no almost sure convergence.

(ii) Spike sequence Xₙ = n·1{U < 1/n}, U uniform. For each ω with U > 0, Xₙ(ω) = 0 once n ≥ 1/U: almost sure (hence in probability) convergence to 0; but E Xₙ² = n → ∞, so not in L² (E Xₙ = 1 does not tend to 0 either: a.s. convergence does not give convergence of the expectation).

(iii) Distribution only. Let X ~ N(0,1) and Xₙ = −X. Each Xₙ has the same law as X, so Xₙ →ᵈ X, but Xₙ − X = −2X never shrinks: P(|Xₙ − X| > 1) = P(|X| > ½) = for all n (not in probability).

05Simulation: the CLT as convergence in distribution

Standardized sum Zₙ = (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) for several n, with its histogram against the N(0,1) density and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance D = sup|FZₙ − Φ|. Skewed or discrete laws need larger n (Berry–Esseen: D ≤ C·E|X − μ|³/(σ³√n)); discrete laws also have a floor of order 1/√n from the lattice.

06Why √n? Additivity of variance and independence

Var(Sₙ) = Σᵢ Var(Xᵢ) + 2 Σ_{i<j} Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) independent (or just uncorrelated): Var(Sₙ) = nσ², sd(Sₙ) = σ√n, Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n, sd(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n
  • Standard deviations do not add: variances do, so the total grows like √n while the mean grows like n. The signal-to-noise ratio of Sₙ is nμ/(σ√n) = √n·μ/σ.
  • Hence the right normalization of the centered sum is √n: Var((Sₙ − nμ)/√n) = σ² for every n, a non-degenerate limit. Dividing by n (LLN scale) gives a limit with zero variance (a constant); dividing by less than √n, or not at all, gives variance that explodes. √n is the only scale at which the fluctuations neither vanish nor diverge.
  • Statistical inference. The standard error σ/√n gives confidence intervals x̄ ± 1.96·s/√n and z/t tests; halving the error requires four times more data (n ≈ (zσ/δ)² for a margin δ); regular estimators are "√n-consistent", √n(θ̂ − θ) →ᵈ N(0, V); the Berry–Esseen error and Donsker's scaling S⌊nt⌋/√n → Brownian motion also use √n.
  • Role of independence. If the covariances do not vanish, the argument fails. For equicorrelated variables (common factor, correlation ρ > 0) Var(Sₙ) = nσ²(1 + (n−1)ρ) grows like n², so sd(X̄ₙ) → σ√ρ ≠ 0: the average does not concentrate (LLN fails), because the common factor never averages out; the effective sample size is about 1/ρ, not n. In security terms: correlated systems sharing the same vulnerability do not give independent evidence.
Part B · Conditional probability, Bayes and inference

07Theory

  • Conditional probability. P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B) for P(B) > 0: the probability of A restricted to the world where B occurred. It gives the product rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)P(B) and the law of total probability P(B) = Σᵢ P(B | Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) for a partition {Aᵢ}. A and B are independent iff P(A | B) = P(A). In statistics it defines models: the distribution of data given a parameter, p(x | θ), or of an outcome given covariates.
  • Bayes' theorem reverses the conditioning:
P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B), in inference: p(θ | x) = p(x | θ) · π(θ) / p(x), p(x) = ∫ p(x | θ) π(θ) dθ posterior ∝ likelihood × prior (odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio)
  • Prior π(θ): what is believed about the parameter before seeing data (base rates, expert knowledge, earlier studies, or a weakly informative default).
  • Likelihood L(θ; x) = p(x | θ): how probable the observed data are for each θ, as a function of θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ (it need not integrate to 1) and is the only way the data enter the posterior.
  • Posterior p(θ | x): the updated belief. The denominator p(x) (the evidence or marginal likelihood) only normalizes. The posterior of one batch of data can serve as the prior of the next, so learning is sequential and order-independent for exchangeable data.
  • Why updating is central. It answers the question practitioners actually ask, "how probable is the hypothesis/parameter given what I observed?", yields direct probability statements and credible intervals, combines prior information with data, works with little data, and handles decisions through expected loss. Frequentist inference instead gives P(data | θ) (p-values, confidence intervals) and treats θ as fixed.

08Example 1: an intrusion detection alert (base-rate effect)

The prior is the prevalence of attacks, the likelihoods are the sensitivity P(alert | attack) and the false-positive rate P(alert | benign). Bayes gives P(attack | alert).

09Example 2: Beta–Binomial, the effect of the prior

Unknown probability θ that an employee clicks a phishing link; data: k clicks in n emails. Likelihood L(θ) ∝ θᵏ(1−θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ; with a Beta(a, b) prior the posterior is exactly Beta(a + k, b + n − k) (conjugacy), with posterior mean (a + k)/(a + b + n): a compromise between prior mean a/(a + b) and the MLE k/n, weighted by prior strength a + b versus sample size n.

10Sequential updating

Trials arrive one at a time with a true click probability θ; after each trial every posterior becomes the next prior. Posterior means under the four priors converge to the truth (and to each other) as data accumulate, the strong wrong prior just needs much more data.

11Discussion: advantages, limitations, influence on conclusions

  • Advantages. Direct probability statements about θ and hypotheses; natural use of prior information (base rates, previous incidents, expert elicitation); coherent sequential updating; sensible answers with little data (zero clicks does not imply θ = 0); credible intervals with a plain interpretation; the same machinery for prediction and decisions.
  • Limitations. The prior is a modelling choice and can be subjective; "flat" priors are not invariant to reparametrization (uniform on θ is not uniform on log-odds); a strong prior can dominate small samples (the "strong and wrong" prior above); conclusions can be sensitive to the likelihood model, which may be misspecified; for non-conjugate models the posterior needs MCMC or variational methods; the evidence p(x) is hard to compute.
  • Influence on conclusions. With little data the posterior is shaped mostly by the prior: the four priors give visibly different answers for n = 20 but agree for n in the thousands (table in section 09), where the likelihood dominates and Bayesian and frequentist answers coincide (Bernstein–von Mises). A sound practice is a sensitivity analysis: report results under several reasonable priors. In the IDS example the prior (prevalence) matters more than the test quality: even a 99%-sensitive test with 1% false positives gives mostly false alarms when attacks are rare, the base-rate fallacy that comes from ignoring the prior.
